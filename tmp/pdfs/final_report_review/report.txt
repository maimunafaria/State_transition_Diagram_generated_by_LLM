1. Introduction 
This chapter presents and analyses the empirical results of the study on generating UML state transition 
diagrams from natural-language requirements using open-source large language models (LLMs). The 
evaluation covers the effects of prompting strategies, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), automated 
syntactic and structural validation, iterative repair, semantic metrics, generation stability, and LLM-as-a-Judge 
evaluation. The chapter first reports the original generation and repair findings, then presents the extension 
comparing baseline repair with syntax-grounded repair and examining the alignment of three LLM judges with 
human evaluators. 
The analysis is organised around three complementary notions of quality. First, syntactic validity determines 
whether the generated PlantUML code can be parsed. Second, structural validity checks graph-level UML 
constraints, including lifecycle, reachability, duplicate transitions, and special-node rules. Third, semantic 
quality examines whether the diagram represents the intended behaviour through State F1, human judgment, 
and LLM-based judgment. This separation is necessary because a diagram can compile successfully and 
satisfy structural rules while still omitting or contradicting required behaviour. 
2. Experimental Setup 
2.1 Dataset and Split 
The benchmark contains 80 requirement–diagram pairs collected from software-engineering textbooks and 
UML instructional materials. Each item includes a structured natural-language requirement and a 
corresponding ground-truth PlantUML state transition diagram. Twenty-seven cases were selected as the 
held-out test set, while the remaining 53 cases were used only for prompting examples and retrieval support. 
The test cases were excluded from the example pool and the RAG corpus to reduce information leakage. 
The dataset covers simple, medium, and complex diagrams. Complexity was operationalised using the number 
of states: simple diagrams contain at most six states, medium diagrams contain seven to fourteen states, and 
complex diagrams contain more than fourteen states. These thresholds correspond to the 25th and 75th 
percentiles of the dataset distribution.

2.3 Validation and Repair 
Every generated output was checked using the PlantUML command-line validator and a deterministic 
rule-based graph analyser. The structural analyser verifies exactly one initial transition, at least one final 
transition, absence of duplicate transitions, reachability, absence of orphan states, valid choice/fork/join 
behavior, valid use of history states, and reliable graph extraction. 
At first, we used a validation-aware repair workflow in which detected syntax and structural violations were 
supplied to the same LLM that generated the diagram. Then we compare this baseline repair prompt with a 
syntax-grounded repair prompt that provides targeted guidance relevant to the detected violation. Both repair 
methods were applied to the same set of 108 cases, enabling paired comparison. 
2.4 Human and LLM Evaluation 
Only diagrams that passed both syntactic and structural validation were submitted for semantic quality 
evaluation. Human evaluators rated four criteria on a five-point Likert scale: completeness, correctness, 
understandability, and terminological alignment. Scores below five required a short justification. 
Three LLM judges evaluated the same requirement–diagram pairs using the same rubric content. DeepSeek 
and Llama evaluated all four criteria in one structured JSON response. Prometheus evaluated one criterion per 
call using its rubric-based absolute-grading format. The LLM judges were not used as formal syntax or 
structural validators; their role was to approximate human semantic assessment after automated filtering.

4. Experimental Results 
The research questions are  
RQ1: How do zero-shot, one-shot, few-shot, and RAG strategies affect the syntactic and structural validity of 
generated state transition diagrams? 
RQ2: How do different RAG knowledge-base configurations affect diagram validity? 
RQ3: How effective is iterative repair in improving syntactic and structural validity? 
RQ4: How do different prompting strategies in the repair phase handle errors and improve strict repair validity 
and error-resolution rate compared with baseline repair? 
RQ5: To what extent do generated diagrams satisfy correctness, completeness, understandability, and 
terminological alignment? 
RQ6: To what extent do LLM judges agree with human evaluators and with one another when assessing 
generated diagrams?

5. Comparative Analysis 
The proposed framework is compared with its own baselines and with major families of prior UML-generation 
approaches. Direct numerical comparison with published systems is not valid because prior studies use 
different diagram types, datasets, prompts, and evaluation rules. 
6. Discussion of Findings 
 
1.​ Finding 1: PlantUML examples provide essential guidance for syntactic validity. Zero-shot prompting 
produced poor syntax, while one-shot and few-shot examples sharply improved PlantUML compilation. 
Few-shot prompting consolidated the improvement and produced the strongest average syntactic validity. 
Results and Analysis  |  16 

===== PAGE 17 =====
 
2.​ Finding 2: Structural validity remains low across prompting strategies. Even when few-shot or RAG 
outputs compiled successfully, strict structural validity remained below 30% in the initial experiments. 
Missing initial or final states and unreachable states were recurring causes of failure. 
3.​ Finding 3: A small set of violation types repeats across many invalid diagrams. Most invalid outputs 
contained only a few violations, but the same violations appeared repeatedly. This suggests systematic 
omissions of critical rules rather than random failure. 
4.​ Finding 4: RAG is not uniformly reliable and its best knowledge source is model-dependent. 
Rules-only RAG worked best for LLaMA and Qwen, the full corpus was comparatively stronger for 
Mistral, and theory-only retrieval was consistently ineffective. Similar but behaviorally different examples 
could also cause retrieval-induced hallucination. 
5.​ Finding 5: More repair iterations do not always improve the result. Most diagrams that became valid 
did so within the first two iterations. Later iterations added few or no successful repairs, indicating 
diminishing returns beyond two or three attempts. 
6.​ Finding 6: Repair is effective for diagrams with fewer issues but struggles with many interacting 
issues. Localized violations were often repaired successfully, whereas diagrams containing multiple faults 
rarely became valid after several attempts. Aggressive correction could remove required states or 
transitions. 
7.​ Finding 7: Repair success varies by violation type and model. DeepSeek repaired a broad range of 
localized violations but struggled with unreachable states. Other models showed narrower strengths, such 
as Mistral on duplicate transitions and Qwen on missing final states. 
8.​ Finding 8: Incorrect diagrams can still be highly understandable. Human evaluators often rated 
readability and understandability higher than correctness. Clear layout and sensible terminology can 
therefore create false confidence when important behavioral flows are missing. 
9.​ Finding 9: Few-shot prompting provides better behavioral quality than RAG. Although RAG 
sometimes improved syntax, few-shot outputs were generally rated more favorably for correctness and 
completeness because fixed examples introduced less irrelevant behavior. 
10.​Finding 10: Syntactic validity is necessary but insufficient. A diagram may compile perfectly while 
violating lifecycle, reachability, or structural requirements. PlantUML validation alone cannot establish 
reliable behavioral modeling. 
11.​Finding 11: Structural validity does not guarantee human-perceived correctness. Some diagrams 
passed every deterministic structural rule but still received low human correctness scores because required 
transitions or behaviors were absent. 
12.​Finding 12: Iterative repair is the strongest overall technique among the evaluated workflows. Repair 
produced the largest gains in automated structural validity, and repaired diagrams that passed validation 
generally received strong human ratings. Targeted validation feedback made repair more dependable than 
single-pass generation. 
13.​Finding 13: Repair effectiveness is model-dependent. Models that performed poorly during initial 
generation also achieved lower repair success. A repair loop cannot fully compensate for weaknesses in a 
model’s understanding of state-machine behavior. 
Practical Recommendations 
●​ Use three to four representative few-shot examples spanning simple, medium, and complex diagrams. 
●​ Apply deterministic validation and limit iterative repair to approximately two or three attempts because 
later iterations show diminishing returns. 
●​ Use automated checks as filters, but retain human review for semantic correctness and completeness, 
especially in safety-critical settings.

7. Threats to Validity and Limitations 
The research identifies several potential threats to the validity of the findings. 
7.1 Construct Validity 
While PlantUML syntactic and structural validity offer a quantitative measure of diagram correctness, they 
may not capture a diagram’s practical usefulness in development workflows. Human evaluation metrics, 
including correctness, completeness, understandability, and terminology alignment, complement the automated 
metrics and provide a more comprehensive quality assessment. 
7.2 Internal Validity 
he main internal threat is variability in LLM responses based on prompt construction. A standardized prompt 
template was utilized for all experiments. The non-deterministic nature of autoregressive generation can 
produce outliers. We addressed this by running each generation once per configuration and focusing on 
systematic patterns across 27 test cases per condition. 
7.3 External Validity 
External validity is limited due to the use of textbook case studies, which may not fully represent the 
complexities found in actual industrial documents. Textbook examples offer a controlled baseline, but 
real-world requirements are often messy and incomplete. Future work should evaluate the proposed workflows 
using industrial requirements. 
7.4 Human Evaluator Bias 
Human evaluation introduces subjectivity, especially when assessing behavioral correctness and 
understandability. To mitigate this threat, the diagrams were independently evaluated by two evaluators with 
experience in UML using predefined criteria and scoring guidelines. Inter-rater agreement was measured using 
Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, with values ranging from κ = 0.44 to κ = 0.68 across the evaluation dimensions. 
Disagreements were resolved through consensus. 
7.5 Reliability of Reconstructed Requirements 
Some requirements were partially reconstructed from textbook diagrams because the original specifications 
were missing or incomplete. Despite manual verification and consistency checks, this reconstruction process 
may have introduced implicit assumptions that were not present in the original sources. 
7.6 Generality of Validation Heuristics 
Some structural validation rules are based on practical modeling heuristics rather than strict UML standards. 
Although these heuristics improve the automated assessment of diagram quality, alternative interpretations of 
the rules may exist. The complete validation rule set is available in the artifact repository for replication and 
adaptation.

7.7 LLM Training Data Contamination 
A significant threat is that the evaluated LLMs may have been pre-trained using the same textbooks and UML 
resources from which the dataset was constructed. If the models had previously encountered the case-study 
diagrams during training, their performance could be inflated because of memorization. Although this 
possibility cannot be completely ruled out, three measures reduce the likelihood that memorization alone 
explains the results. First, structured reformulations of the requirements were used instead of the original 
textbook wording. Second, the models were required to generate diagrams in PlantUML syntax, which was 
unlikely to appear verbatim in the source materials. Third, the poor zero-shot performance across all models, 
including 0% structural validity, indicates that prior exposure alone was insufficient because the models still 
failed without explicit prompting. Future work should validate the findings using newly created and non-public 
requirements. 
8. Summary 
This study presents a validation-aware framework for generating UML state transition diagrams from natural 
language requirements using prompting, RAG, automated validation, iterative repair, LLM-based judging, and 
stacked ensemble selection. Four open-source LLMs were evaluated on a dataset of 80 requirement–diagram 
pairs. 
Few-shot prompting substantially improved syntactic validity, but structural validity remained low because of 
recurring issues such as missing initial or final states, unreachable states, and orphan states. RAG performance 
was model- and corpus-dependent; rules-only retrieval helped some models, while theory-only retrieval was 
generally ineffective and retrieved examples sometimes introduced unsupported behavior. 
Iterative repair produced the strongest improvements. Syntax-grounded repair increased strict repair validity 
from 53.70% to 58.33% and the error-resolution rate from 53.42% to 62.73%. However, repair was most 
effective for small, localized errors and offered limited gains for diagrams with multiple interacting violations. 
The LLM-as-a-judge results showed only moderate alignment with human evaluation. DeepSeek achieved the 
best overall agreement, with an MAE of 0.655, 50.77% exact agreement, and weighted kappa of 0.401. 
Inter-judge agreement was also limited, and possible self-evaluation bias was observed. Therefore, LLM 
judges can support large-scale evaluation but should not replace human assessment. 
The stacked ensemble selected candidates from different models and strategies, showing that no single 
configuration performed best for every case. Baseline repair was selected most often, followed by 
syntax-grounded repair, while DeepSeek was the most frequently selected generator. 
Overall, reliable AI-assisted behavioral modeling requires a combined workflow of few-shot prompting, 
deterministic validation, targeted repair, multi-model selection, and human review. LLMs are therefore better 
suited as collaborative modeling assistants than as fully autonomous generators.
