Abstract
7
Background: Generating behavioral models, such as state transition diagrams, from natural
8
language requirements presents challenges in requirements analysis and design. Traditional NLP
9
and ML methods struggle to maintain the correct execution flow and structural consistency. Recent
10
advances in LLMs offer new opportunities, though their effectiveness in generating behavioral
11
models—particularly concerning prompting, retrieval, and repair strategies—remains largely unex-
12
amined. Aims: This paper evaluates how LLM-based generation strategies (zero-shot, one-shot,
13
few-shot, and RAG) impact the quality dimensions (correctness, completeness, understandability,
14
and terminological alignment) of state transition diagrams.
It also explores whether iterative
15
repair mechanisms enhance validity. Method: We created a dataset of 80 requirement–diagram
16
pairs from software engineering textbooks, featuring structured requirements and corresponding
17
PlantUML state transition diagrams. For each requirement, we generated PlantUML code using
18
four prompting strategies across four open-source LLMs. To isolate the effect of different knowledge
19
sources, we conducted a RAG analysis study comparing four retrieval corpus configurations (full
20
corpus, examples-only, rules-only, and theory-only). We additionally assessed the effectiveness of an
21
iterative repair workflow using automated evaluation to improve the validity of generated PlantUML
22
code. The code was evaluated for syntactic validity through PlantUML validation and structural
23
validity using rule-based analysis, supplemented by human evaluation of quality dimensions. In
24
total, we produced 1,169 PlantUML code outputs, of which 120 outputs that passed both validations
25
were manually evaluated by human experts. Results: Few-shot prompting outperformed zero-shot
26
methods, improving syntactic validity from 28.7% to 93.5% on average. Iterative repair significantly
27
improved structural validity, especially for DeepSeek (from 22.2% to 88.9%), with most repairs
28
succeeding in the first two iterations. Human evaluation indicated that understandability was rated
29
higher than correctness, highlighting a gap between perceived and actual quality. Conclusions: We
30
recommend using few-shot prompting with iterative repair as the primary workflow for AI-assisted
31
behavioral modeling. Our findings reveal critical trade-offs: retrieval improves syntax but risks
32
hallucination, model architecture matters more than strategy, and automated validation cannot
33
substitute for human correctness assessment. To our knowledge, this is the first empirical study
34
of LLM-based UML state transition diagram generation from natural language requirements that
35
systematically evaluates prompting, retrieval analysis, and repair workflows.

Introduction
39
Requirements engineering bridges stakeholder aspirations and technical software implementa-
40
tion by relying primarily on natural language requirements, which remain widely used due
41
to their flexibility and accessibility for non-technical stakeholders [48]. However, natural
42
language often introduces lexical, syntactic, and semantic ambiguities [45], necessitating
43
rigorous requirements analysis and modeling. Graphical modeling languages like the Unified
44
Modeling Language (UML) [40] provide structured representations of system behavior [33].
45

UML diagrams are categorized into structural models and behavioral models [2, 47]. Among
46
behavioral models, state transition diagrams are crucial for modeling discrete system states
47
and event-driven transitions. These diagrams are essential in safety-critical applications, such
48
as healthcare, financial processing, embedded systems, and robotics[41], where state logic
49
errors can lead to significant failures. Yet, constructing these diagrams manually is labor-
50
intensive and error-prone, as analysts must infer states, transitions, and guards implicitly
51
embedded in text—a task more challenging than automating structural models [12]. Previous
52
automation attempts, using Natural Language Processing (NLP) or machine learning (ML),
53
failed to achieve the necessary contextual reasoning for global model consistency [61].
54
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) enable automation in behavioral soft-
55
ware modeling through their ability to process long inputs and capture semantic relationships
56
across requirements [6]. Studies show LLMs effectively support software engineering tasks
57
like code generation and requirements analysis [32, 51, 54, 53, 16, 13, 8, 1, 58]. However,
58
most research has focused on structural UML models, leaving the generation of behavioral
59
models, particularly UML state transition diagrams, underexplored [26, 60].
60
Behavioral diagrams must preserve global execution semantics [11], including state
61
reachability, transition consistency, branching behavior, and lifecycle correctness [25] across
62
the entire model. Consequently, the challenge lies in not just generating valid diagrams
63
but ensuring they are structurally consistent, behaviorally correct, and comprehensible to
64
practitioners. In practice, generated diagrams often require verification and refinement,
65
making the reliability of AI-assisted modeling contingent on the LLM and the surrounding
66
workflow, which includes prompting strategies, retrieval support, automated validation, and
67
iterative repair. Our study examines how different AI-assisted generation workflows affect the
68
reliability of synthesizing UML state transition diagrams from natural language requirements.
69
In this paper, we present a dataset and an empirical framework to systematically evaluate
70
LLM-based generation of UML state transition diagrams from natural language requirements.
71
The dataset consists of curated requirement–diagram pairs derived from software engineering
72
textbook sources, including structured requirements and corresponding PlantUML [42]
73
representations. We evaluate multiple open-source LLMs (Llama-3.1-8B [14], DeepSeek-
74
14B [23], Mistral 7B Instruct [31], and Qwen-2.5-Coder-7B [27]), chosen for their use in
75
recent literature [15, 57, 62], across generation strategies, including structured prompting
76
[56], Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and a iterative repair workflow for iterative
77
refinement.
We introduce diagram-specific metrics such as syntax validity (PlantUML
78
validation), structural validity (rule based analysis of the diagram detecting orphan states,
79
unreachable states, invalid transitions, etc), and quality evaluation through human evaluation
80
(correctness, completeness, understandability and technological alignment), which enables
81
assessment of the diagrams and workflow.
82
The research questions driving this study focus on understanding the behavior of LLMs
83
in software modeling tasks. While LLMs can generate diagrams from requirements, the
84
impact of different prompting and retrieval strategies on structural correctness and reliability
85
remains unclear. Generating valid state transition diagrams requires identifying states and
86
transitions while ensuring global properties like reachability and consistency. To investigate
87
the effect of generation strategies on structural correctness, we first ask:
88

89
impact the validity (syntactic and structural) of state transition diagrams?
90
Recent studies suggest that RAG may improve LLM performance by incorporating relevant
91
contextual information; however, its effectiveness in structured diagram generation remains
92
underexplored [55, 19]. To evaluate its impact, we analyze how retrieval augmentation

2
Background and Related Work
116
2.1
Rule-Based and Symbolic NLP Approaches
117
Early automated software architectural model derivations utilized symbolic NLP techniques
118
like part-of-speech tagging and syntax parsing [59]. ArchE [5] was one of the first systems
119
to use predefined architectural reasoning rules for software design. Similarly, frameworks
120
like aToucan [59] applied handcrafted linguistic patterns to extract entities and actions
121
from use case models and map them to UML elements. Jahan et al. [29] enhanced rule-
122
based approaches with heuristic mappings for generating sequence diagrams from textual
123
requirements. While effective for structured requirements, these approaches were difficult to
124
generalize across domains. Most notably, rule-based systems struggled to capture implicit
125
behavioral dependencies and global execution semantics needed for modeling behaviors in
126
UML state transition diagrams [59, 29, 30]. Consequently, symbolic approaches remained
127
limited to requirement extraction tasks and structural UML models.
128
2.2
Machine Learning for Software Architectural Model Extraction
129
To address the limitations of rule-based systems, later work reframed software model ex-
130
traction as ML problem using classification and sequence-labeling techniques [30]. These
131
approaches utilized lexical, syntax, and semantic features to identify software entities and
132
relationships from natural language requirements. However, the effectiveness of traditional
133
ML approaches was constrained by the limited availability of large annotated datasets in
134
software engineering [30]. In addition, classical ML models often lacked the contextual
135
reasoning capabilities required to preserve behavioral consistency across an entire state
136

transition diagram[30, 39]. Consequently, most prior work focused on extracting structural
137
relationships rather than generating behaviorally consistent UML models.
138
2.3
LLMs in Requirements Engineering and UML Modeling
139
LLMs have transformed requirements engineering by enabling complex reasoning without
140
extensive manual feature engineering [17, 52].
Studies show LLMs assist in ambiguity
141
detection, requirement refinement, and information extraction from Software Requirements
142
Specifications (SRS) [36]. Frameworks like REQINONE [62] and ReqBrain [24] demonstrate
143
that LLMs can convert natural language requirements into structured representations by
144
task specific framework tuning, enhancing clarity and completeness. Research has also
145
focused on UML model generation using LLMs, particularly for structural artifacts like class
146
and sequence diagrams [17, 52, 10]. LLMs generally produce higher-quality diagrams when
147
relationships are clearly described in the requirements [29, 30, 17, 57]. Various approaches
148
aim to enhance AI-assisted software modeling workflows. Cervantes et al. [9] integrate the
149
Attribute-Driven Design (ADD) methodology into LLM interactions for better alignment with
150
expert architectural solutions. Multi-agent systems like NOMAD [20] decompose UML class
151
diagram generation into specialized subtasks for improved modularity. Other studies propose
152
hybrid workflows combining LLM generation with static analysis and verification tools for
153
increased reliability [28]. Despite these advancements, limitations persist. Most studies focus
154
on structural models, leaving behavioral model generation underexplored [17, 52, 10, 15].
155
Generated diagrams are often evaluated through subjective inspection or syntax correctness,
156
with limited attention to behavioral consistency [52, 39, 28]. Many approaches still rely
157
heavily on manual refinement and expert intervention to correct artifacts [52, 36, 9]. Lastly,
158
prior studies lack standardized empirical evaluation frameworks for systematically comparing
159
prompting, retrieval, and repair workflows [30, 17, 52, 15].
160
2.4
Motivation
161
Existing research show the potential of LLMs for requirements engineering and structural
162
model generation [17, 52, 62, 24]. However, important gaps remain for behavioral modeling
163
tasks. Prior work focuses on structural UML artifacts like class and sequence diagrams
164
[17, 52, 10, 15], while behavioral models require maintaining global execution semantics and
165
behavioral consistency across the entire diagram [39, 28]. Moreover, existing approaches rely
166
on manual refinement and oversight [52, 36, 9], provide limited automated validation checking
167
[52, 50, 28], and lack standardized empirical evaluation frameworks for comparing AI-assisted
168
modeling workflows [30, 17, 52, 15]. Additionally, past evaluations treat diagram generation
169
as an isolated task instead of part of a comprehensive software engineering workflow [28, 15],
170
leading to a limited understanding of how workflow components affect the validity of AI-
171
assisted behavioral modeling. Motivated by these limitations, this study systematically
172
evaluates prompting strategies, retrieval augmentation, validation-aware repair workflows,
173
and automated and quality evaluation for UML state transition diagram generation from
174
natural language requirements.

3.1
Dataset Preparation
182
To the best of our knowledge, no existing dataset with requirement-diagram pairs was
183
available as ground truth for state transition diagram generation. Therefore, we construc-
184
ted a benchmark dataset from 12 software engineering textbooks and UML instructional
185
materials [49, 46, 43, 34, 48, 37, 40, 4, 22, 21, 18, 7]. Textbooks were chosen because they:
186
(A) provide curated examples of system behavior reflecting standard modeling practices;
187
(B) feature clear behavioral logic, essential for reliable ground truth; and (C) are publicly
188
accessible and reproducible, aligning with empirical software engineering standards.
Figure 1 Behavioral Modeling Dataset Construction Workflow
189
For each data point, we extracted the state transition diagram in PNG format and its
190
corresponding natural language requirements in TXT format (Fig. 1-A, 1-B). However, many
191
natural language requirements were incomplete or absent, with only diagrams provided in
192
the books. We addressed this inconsistency by updating the requirements to align with
193
the diagrams (Fig. 1-C). In case of absent requirements, we manually reconstructed the
194
requirements from the diagram. In case a diagram introduced states or transitions not present
195
in the requirements, we added the missing requirements.
196
After aligning the requirements with the diagrams, we standardized the requirements
197
text of each diagram into a structured requirements format (Fig. 1-D). We represented each
198
requirements text as a list of individual requirements so that it is easier and clearer for the
199
LLM to understand when used in prompts. Finally, we generated a corresponding diagram
200
file in PlantUML [42] format (Fig. 1-E). We used PlantUML because its text-based format
201
supports automated syntactic checks, reproducible rendering, and use in software engineering
202
workflows [38]. Each diagram was verified for syntax through PlantUML code compilation
203
and assessed for correctness against the requirements.
204
Each final data point consists of five artifacts: raw requirements text (unstructured natural
205
language), aligned requirements text (used when requirements were missing or incomplete),
206
structured requirements text (requirements in a standardized list format), book diagram (dia-
207
gram in PNG format from the textbook), and PlantUML code (ground truth state transition
208
diagram in textual UML format). The final dataset contains 80 requirement-diagram pairs.
209
All five artifacts for each case are provided in the companion repository [3]. By addressing
210
data incompleteness, enforcing semantic alignment, and standardizing requirements and
211
diagram representations, our dataset construction process supports reproducible and con-
212
sistent evaluation of LLM-based state transition diagram generation, following empirical
213
principles of software engineering [44]. We maintained multiple artifact representations to
214
ensure traceability and flexibility for future research.
215
To map diagrams with different levels of complexity, we stratified the data points by the
216
number of states in each diagram. The stratification thresholds were empirically derived

3.2
Diagram Generation Using Different Prompting Strategies
222
For our empirical exploration of state transition diagram generation, we used four open-
223
source LLMs: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [14], DeepSeek-R1-14B [23], Mistral-7B-Instruct [31],
224
and Qwen-2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct [27]. We selected these models based on their recent use in
225
software engineering literature [15, 57, 62] and their availability for reproducible research.
226
From the dataset, we randomly selected 27 data points as the test dataset, using the
227
remaining 53 for prompting and retrieval. For each requirement in the test dataset, we
228
generated PlantUML code using three prompting strategies:
229
Zero-shot: Only structured requirements text was provided with task instructions.
230
One-shot: Structured requirements text, task instructions, and one requirement-diagram
231
example outside the test dataset were provided.
232
Few-shot: Structured requirements text, task instructions, and three examples outside
233
the test dataset were provided. One example was selected from each complexity category:
234
simple, medium, and complex.
235
The exact prompt templates for each strategy are provided in the artifact repository [3].
236

3.3
Diagram Evaluation
256
Evaluating the quality of generated UML state transition diagrams is inherently multi-faceted.
257
Some aspects can be assessed automatically, such as syntactic validity, which checks whether
258
the PlantUML code follows the language grammar, and structural validity, which checks
259
whether the diagram follows UML state transition diagram rules. However, other aspects
260
require human judgment, such as whether the diagram correctly captures the intended
261
behavior, is complete with respect to the requirements, is understandable to a human,
262
and uses terminology consistently with the requirements. Therefore, we used a two-stage
263
evaluation process. First, we performed automated evaluation of syntactic and structural
264
validity for every generated diagram. Then, we conducted human evaluation on a subset
265
of diagrams that passed both automated stages. For human evaluation, we used quality
266
dimensions used by other researchers for UML diagram evaluation [57, 38].
267
3.3.1
Automated Evaluation
268
Automated evaluation consists of two stages: syntactic validity and structural validity.
269
Both stages were applied to every generated PlantUML diagram before human assessment.
270
syntactic validity was assessed using the PlantUML command-line tool with the plantuml
271
-checkonly flag. If the code is correct, it completes without errors; if there are violations,
272
the tool reports them. A diagram is syntacticly valid only if no errors are reported.
273
Diagrams that pass this check are evaluated for structural validity using our rule-based
274
graph analyzer. First, the PlantUML code is normalized by removing markdown fences and
275
extracting the @startuml ... @enduml block to retain only the diagram code. This normalized
276
code is parsed into a graph representation, capturing states, transitions, starting points,
277
ending states, and special elements. The analyzer stores each state and records transitions,
278
along with source states, destination states, and transition labels or conditions. It also tracks
279
the initial state, final states, and special elements like choice, fork, join, and history states.
280
The rule-based graph analyzer checks the graph against the structural rules in Table 1,
281
each implemented as a deterministic check. A diagram passes structural validity only if all
282
rules are satisfied; otherwise, the specific violation types are recorded.
283
The row (parse_warning) captures cases where the PlantUML code is syntacticly valid but
284
contains ambiguities or non-standard constructs that hinder reliable graph extraction. The
285
implementation of the rule-based graph analyzer is available in the artifact repository [3].
286

287
Diagrams that passed both syntactic and structural validation were further assessed through
288

289

291
following established practices for evaluating generated UML models [57, 38]:
292
Completeness: Whether all required states, transitions, events, guards, and initial/final
293
states described in the requirements are present in the generated diagram.

3.4
Iterative Repair Workflow
312
To improve the syntactic and structural validity of generated diagrams, we explored an
313
iterative repair workflow. This workflow was applied using the strongest initial strategy
314

Anonymous author(s)
9
for each LLM, i.e., the strategy that produced the highest number of syntacticly and
315
structurally valid diagrams during the initial automated evaluation (see Section 4 for the
316
per-LLM ranking). For each selected strategy, only diagrams from the test dataset that
317
failed automated evaluation entered the iterative repair workflow. The repair process follows
318
the looping mechanism illustrated in Figure 3:
319
1. Evaluation and Issue Collection – The candidate PlantUML diagram is checked
320
using automated evaluation. This includes syntactic validity checking with plantuml
321
-checkonly and structural validity checking using the rule-based graph analyzer described
322
in Section 3.3.1. All detected issues, including syntactic error messages and structural
323
violation types from Table 1, are collected into a list.
324
2. LLM Repair – The same LLM used for generation receives a repair prompt with
325
instructions and identified issues, then generates new PlantUML code.
326
Figure 3 Validation-aware iterative repair workflow
3. Re-evaluation and Acceptance – The repaired output is normalized and re-evaluated
327
for syntactic and structural validity. If it reduces the recorded validation issues, it replaces
328
the current candidate. Otherwise, the previous version is retained.
329
4. Iteration – The repair loop is repeated while validity issues remain or repair attempts
330
are still available. The loop stops when the diagram passes all checks or the maximum
331
repair attempt limit is reached.
332
5. Final Output – After the repair loop ends, the best accepted version is saved as the
333
final diagram. This is either the first version that passes all checks or the version with
334
the fewest validity violations.
335
The repair instruction is deliberately conservative. It asks the model to modify only the
336
lines necessary to fix the listed issues, avoid redesigning the diagram, and refrain from adding
337
unsupported states or transitions. This was designed to prevent the LLM from hallucinating
338
large behavioral changes while still allowing targeted corrections. The effectiveness of this
339
workflow is evaluated by comparing the automated and human evaluation scores of the
340
repaired diagrams against their initial counterparts. Results are presented in Section 4. The
341
full repair workflow configuration, including prompt, iteration logging and acceptance criteria,
342
is available in the artifact repository [3].

RQ1: Impact of Prompting Strategies on Validity
345

346

RQ2: Retrieval Corpus Analysis – Which Retrieval Source Helps?
367
To understand how different document sources in the retrieval corpus affect diagram validity,
368
we evaluated RAG using three separate settings: examples only, rules only, and theory only.
369

371
Finding 4: RAG is not uniformly reliable; different sources work best for
372
different models. For instruction-tuned models (Llama, Qwen), rules-only RAG is most
373
effective (Llama 42.9%, Qwen 50.0%). For Mistral, full RAG (examples + rules + theory)
374
gives the best result (20.0%), while examples-only (4.2%) and rules-only (5.6%) perform
375
poorly. Theory-only RAG is uniformly ineffective (0–5% structural validity). Thus, retrieval
376

377

378
human evaluator noted that "requirements 3–6 are missing but adding new things." Upon
379
inspection, we found that the retrieved example was textually similar to the target requirement
380
but behaviorally different. The generated diagram omitted required states and transitions
381
from the target case and included additional ones from the retrieved example, indicating
382
a retrieval-induced hallucination where the model borrows unsupported behavior from a
383
relevant but different example.
384
RQ2 Takeaway: Reliability of retrieval document source depends on the model.
Rules-only helps instruction-tuned models whereas theory-only performs very poorly.
Practitioners must tailor retrieval to the target LLM.

Cross-RQ Findings
438
Finding 10: Syntactic validity is necessary but insufficient for behavioral modeling.
439
A diagram can be perfectly valid PlantUML (e.g., Llama RAG 100% syntax) yet fail all
440
structural rules (only 22% structural validity) (as shown in Table 1). Syntax checks give a
441
false sense of security.
442
Finding 11: Structural validity does not guarantee human-perceived correct-
443
ness. We observed cases where diagrams passed all structural rules, such as having a single
444
initial state and no unreachable states, but still received low human correctness scores (2–3)
445
because they missed critical transitions. This shows that automated validation alone is not
446
sufficient and must be supported by human oversight.
447
Finding 12: Repair is the best technique – improves both automated and
448
human evaluation.
DeepSeek achieved the highest repair success rate for structural
449
validity (88.9%), followed by Llama (55.6%), Qwen (40.7%), and Mistral (29.6%) as shown
450
in Figure 6. This improvement is mainly due to targeted feedback: the LLM receives precise
451
structural violation messages, such as missing_final_state, and is instructed to modify only
452
the necessary lines. More importantly, diagrams that passed structural validation after repair
453
were also rated highly by human evaluators across all quality dimensions. This trend held for
454
both few-shot and RAG-based repair settings. Fixing structural issues leads to improving
455
the behavioral quality of the diagram as a byproduct. This suggests that automated success
456
after repair is a strong indicator of human-perceived quality as well. Therefore, repair is
457
not only the most effective strategy for structural validity, but also the most trustworthy
458
strategy for practical use.
459
Finding 13: Repair effectiveness is model-dependent.
Models that struggle
460
with initial structural validity (Mistral, Qwen) also see lower repair success (29.6%, 40.7%)
461
compared to DeepSeek (88.9%).
The repair loop cannot compensate for fundamental
462
limitations in a model’s ability to understand state transition diagram semantics.

4.6
Practical Recommendations for AI-Assisted Behavioral Modeling
464
Based on our findings, we provide three recommendations for practitioners using LLMs for
465
state transition diagram generation.
466
1. Prioritize few-shot prompting. Use 3–4 examples representing simple, medium, and
467
complex cases. Ensure these examples are structurally correct and aligned with the target
468
domain. Few-shot prompting outperforms all other methods in semantic fidelity and
469
reduces retrieval-induced hallucinations.
470
2. Integrate iterative repair. Despite optimal prompting, 70–80% of generated diagrams
471
may have structural violations. Implement an automated repair loop with 2–3 iterations,
472
as additional iterations yield diminishing returns. This lightweight feedback mechanism
473
significantly improves structural validity (e.g., DeepSeek from 25% to 96%).
474

4.7
Limitations and Future Work
480
Despite the comprehensive evaluation, several limitations remain. The dataset, while system-
481
atically constructed, consists of textbook examples that may not capture industrial complexity
482
(concurrency, timing constraints, hierarchical states). The repair loop was capped at 5 iter-
483
ations based on pilot experiments showing no improvements beyond this point, but more
484
iterations might benefit certain cases. Human evaluation, while rigorous, involved only two
485
evaluators; larger-scale validation with diverse practitioners would strengthen generalization.
486
Future work should explore: (a) hierarchical state transition diagrams and orthogonal regions,
487
which introduce additional validation complexity; (b) few-shot example selection optimization
488
(currently random, but semantic similarity-based selection may improve performance); (c)
489
using LLMs as judges to automate or augment human evaluation of diagram quality; (d)
490
providing structural rules themselves as part of the RAG context to assess whether explicit
491
rule injection improves structural validity; and (e) analyzing oscillation and regression during
492
repair—specifically, tracking per-iteration errors, fixes, and fix patterns to understand why
493
some models regress and how to design more stable repair prompts.

5
Threats to Validity
495
The research identifies several potential threats to the validity of the findings.
496
Construct validity: While PlantUML syntactic and structural validity offer a quant-
497
itative measure of diagram correctness, they may not capture a diagram’s practical
498
usefulness in development workflows. Human evaluation metrics (correctness, complete-
499
ness, understandability, terminology alignment) complement automated metrics for a
500
more comprehensive quality assessment.
501

16

Internal validity: The main internal threat is variability in LLM responses based on
502
prompt construction. A standardized prompt template was utilized for all experiments.
503
The non-deterministic nature of autoregressive generation can produce outliers; we ad-
504
dressed this by running each generation once per configuration and focusing on systematic
505
patterns across 27 test cases per condition.
506
External validity: External validity is limited due to the use of textbook case studies,
507
which may not fully represent the complexities found in actual industrial documents.
508
Textbook examples offer a controlled baseline, but real-world requirements are often
509
messy and incomplete. Future work should evaluate workflows on industrial requirements.
510
Human evaluator bias: Human evaluation introduces subjectivity, especially in as-
511
sessing behavioral correctness and understandability. To mitigate this, diagrams were
512
independently evaluated by two UML-experienced evaluators using predefined criteria and
513
scoring guidelines. Inter-rater agreement was measured with Cohen’s Kappa coefficient
514
(κ = 0.44–0.68 across dimensions), with disagreements resolved through consensus.
515
Reliability of reconstructed requirements: Some requirements were partially re-
516
constructed from textbook diagrams due to missing specifications.
Despite manual
517
verification and consistency checks, reconstruction may introduce implicit assumptions
518
not present in the original sources.
519
Generality of validation heuristics: Some structural validation rules are based on
520
practical modeling heuristics rather than strict UML standards. While these heuristics
521
enhance automated diagram quality assessment, alternative interpretations may exist. The
522
full rule set is available in the artifact repository (Sec. 6) for replication and adaptation.
523
LLM training data contamination: A significant threat is that the evaluated LLMs
524
may have been pre-trained on the same textbooks and UML resources as our dataset. If
525
models "saw" the case study diagrams during training, their performance could be inflated
526
due to memorization. While we cannot rule out contamination, we note: (a) we used
527
structured requirement reformulations differing from original textbook wording; (b) we
528
required generation in PlantUML syntax, unlikely to appear verbatim in training corpora;
529
and (c) the poor zero-shot performance across all models (0% structural validity) suggests
530
that memorization alone is insufficient, as models still fail without explicit prompting.
531
Future work should validate findings on newly created, non-public requirements.

6
Conclusion
533
This work contributes one of the first validation-aware empirical frameworks for studying AI-
534
assisted behavioral model generation, combining automated structural verification, retrieval-
535
based prompting, iterative repair, and human-centered evaluation. Using a novel dataset of
536
80 requirement–state transition diagram pairs derived from software engineering textbooks,
537
we evaluated four open-source LLMs across zero-shot, one-shot, few-shot, RAG, and repair
538
workflows. Our key findings are:
539
(1) Few-shot prompting significantly outperforms zero-shot and one-shot for syntactic
540
validity, but structural validity remains low (30%) across all models, with missing final and
541
initial states as common errors. Passing a syntax check alone gives false confidence.
542
(2) RAG’s effectiveness is model-dependent. Rules-only RAG works best for instruction-tuned
543
models (Llama, Qwen), while theory-only RAG is uniformly ineffective. RAG can induce
544
hallucination by copying irrelevant content from retrieved examples.
545
(3) Iterative repair dramatically improves structural validity, especially for DeepSeek
546
(from 22.2% to 88.9%), with most repairs succeeding within the first two iterations. Repair
547

===== PAGE 17 =====
Anonymous author(s)
17
is most effective for small, localized errors; diagrams with many violations rarely become
548
valid. Repair success also varies by violation type—DeepSeek fixes missing final states and
549
missing initial states perfectly but cannot handle unreachable states.
550
(4) Human evaluation shows a disconnect: diagrams are rated high for understandab-
551
ility (often 5/5) but low for correctness (3–4/5). Few-shot prompting aligns better with
552
requirements than RAG, and diagrams passing automated validation after repair receive
553
high human ratings, making repair the most reliable strategy.
554
Taken together, these results demonstrate that reliable AI-assisted behavioral modeling
555
is achievable not through larger prompts or more retrieval, but through targeted, validation-
556
aware workflows that combine few-shot examples with iterative repair. Practitioners can
557
adopt our validation rules and repair prompts to integrate LLMs into their modeling processes
558
as collaborative assistants rather than autonomous generators.
